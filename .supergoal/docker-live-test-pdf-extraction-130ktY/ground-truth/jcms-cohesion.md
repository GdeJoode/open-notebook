# The Centrifugal State: In Search of an Explanation of Why Countries Decentralise

**Author:** Andrés Rodríguez-Pose

**Affiliation:** Department of Geography and Environment and Cañada Blanch Centre, London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, United Kingdom. E-mail: A.Rodriguez-Pose@lse.ac.uk

**Publication:** Rodríguez-Pose A., 2024. *The centrifugal state: In search of an explanation of why countries decentralise.* Quaestiones Geographicae 43(4), Bogucki Wydawnictwo Naukowe, Poznań, pp. 35–48. 3 figs, 1 table. DOI 10.14746/quageo-2024-0041. Open access (Creative Commons Attribution).

**Manuscript:** received November 15, 2024; revised November 21, 2024.

## Abstract

Nation-states across the world are increasingly relinquishing powers to subnational tiers of government. This paper examines the drivers of this decentralisation revolution and the forces propelling it. While conventional wisdom has long attributed the phenomenon to the triumvirate of democracy, economic efficiency, and regional identity, the contemporary dynamics demand a far more nuanced interpretation. Through the dual lenses of the efficiency-based decentralisation theorem and political economy analysis, the author discusses how globalisation, technological upheaval, and innovations in governance have reshaped the relationship between political centres and periphery within countries. The empirical evidence reveals decentralisation not as a linear progression but as an intricate interweaving of economic imperatives and political will, defying simple causation. The paper offers insights into the evolving architecture of multi-level governance while acknowledging the challenge of isolating singular causes.

**Keywords:** decentralisation; democracy; economic efficiency; regional identity; globalisation; political economy; governance reforms; multi-level governance.

## Introduction

Since the 1970s a strong centrifugal force within the nation-state — manifesting as decentralisation — has become a defining feature of contemporary governance, reshaping polities from Britain to Brazil, Spain to China. The central question: why is the global drive towards decentralisation happening, and why now?

Three "grand narratives" have traditionally explained transfers of authority from central to subnational tiers: (1) the **democratic imperative**, (2) the **pursuit of economic efficiency**, and (3) the **persistence of regional identity**. Beneath these lies a more complex reality, in which appeals to democratic principles, public welfare, or ethnic identity often serve as convenient justifications for policies adopted for other reasons.

Two analytical frameworks emerge strongly: one grounded in **welfare economics** (optimal service delivery, economic scale, territorial expanse), and one rooted in **political economy** (decentralisation as the product of competing interests and institutional design). Determinants of optimal decentralisation fall into two broad categories:

- **Static factors:** national size, population distribution, ethnic composition, constitutional traditions (explaining why federal India differs from centralised France).
- **Dynamic factors:** evolving security threats, widening regional disparities, economic integration, the rise of the knowledge economy.

The paper treats decentralisation as a **dynamic spectrum** rather than a federal/unitary binary, positioning factors within a political economy framework.

## The global drive towards decentralisation

The world has become increasingly decentralised. The analysis draws on the **Regional Authority Index (RAI)** dataset compiled by Shair-Rosenfield et al. (2021) and Hooghe et al. The RAI has ten dimensions across two domains — "self-rule" and "shared rule" — including fiscal autonomy, policy scope, and law-making authority. ## The political economy framework

The dominant theory is the **'decentralisation theorem'** (Oates 1972, 2005), distilling the earlier "efficiency approach" of Samuelson (1954, 1955), Tiebout (1956), Musgrave (1959), and Arrow (1969). It matches governmental competencies to their optimal territorial scale, weighing economies of scale (e.g., national defence) against local customisation (e.g., waste collection, employment training). Under specific conditions, varied local provision is Pareto-superior to uniform central delivery (Oates 2005).

Critics argue governance-level decisions arise not from dispassionate cost-benefit analysis but from self- and party-political interest, especially where institutions are weak. Rodden (2006) dismisses decentralisation as flowing naturally from welfare economics. Treisman (2007) argues theoretical efficiency bears little relation to policymakers' actual decisions. Even Oates (2005) acknowledged the need for a **'second-generation' theory of fiscal federalism**. A methodological divide persists between quantitative cross-country analyses (economists) and case studies (political scientists, geographers, historians).

The paper notes two key observations: decentralisation's various forms spring from different sources; and centralisation and decentralisation **coexist** — "two faces of the same political coin" — with the "decentralisation pendulum" constantly swinging (Blankart 2000: decentralisation as a reaction to over-centralisation).

## Static determinants of decentralisation

Static determinants are wealth, territorial expanse, population, and ethnic composition.

- **Constitutions:** change slowly; federal constitutions generally herald higher decentralisation, though the federal/unitary binary dissolves into a continuum (Keating 2013). Germany (federal) can be more centralised than France (unitary) in some areas; France has quietly decentralised this century.
- **Colonial heritage:** British colonial administration seeded local autonomy; some Latin American (Spanish) domains remained centralised; even federal Argentina, Mexico, Venezuela have had centralised realities behind federal facades (Treisman 2007; Falleti 2010).
- **Preference heterogeneity** (fiscal federalism): regional variation in voter preferences should yield more decentralisation (Hooghe et al. 2010).
- **Territorial scale / population density:** larger nations incline toward decentralisation (distance as a barrier); sparse populations may demand it, concentrated urban populations achieve economies of scale (Arzaghi & Henderson 2005; Lessmann & Markwardt 2010; Panizza 1999).
- **Ethnic diversity:** ambiguous — sometimes a "pressure valve" for ethnic tensions (Brancati 2009), sometimes correlated with centralisation (Treisman 2007).
- **Wealth / inequality:** regional income disparities can both favour decentralisation and strengthen the centre's redistributive role; national wealth generally correlates positively with decentralisation (Panizza 1999; Letelier-Saavedra & Sáez-Lozano 2015).

**Bottom line:** larger, more populous, wealthier nations and those with federal constitutions tend to decentralise more; ethnic diversity, inequality, and wealth tell more complex, context-mediated tales.

## Dynamic drivers of decentralisation

Two main dynamic forces: **globalisation** (Ezcurra & Rodríguez-Pose 2013) and **democratisation** (Pius Kulipossa 2004; Enikolopov & Zhuravskaya 2007). Also relevant: economic growth, widening inequality, the shift from industrial to **knowledge-based economies**, the rise of cities, and supranational entities.

- **Economic integration:** international market integration makes smallness less costly (Rodrik 2011); the **EU** is the strongest test case, with multilevel governance empowering regions ("Europe of the Regions"; Hooghe & Marks 2016; Keating 2013). Evidence is ambiguous — some find positive integration–decentralisation links (Stegarescu 2009; Kyriacou & Roca-Sagalés 2011), others find globalisation favours central coordination (Rodrik 2011; Diaz-Cayeros 2006 found trade openness aligned with centralisation).
- **Democratisation:** the World Bank (2012) documents democracy's expansion 1974–2012, coinciding with decentralisation (Spain, Eastern Europe, Latin America). Yet exceptions abound (Estonia's post-democratic centralisation; Zimbabwe, Belarus). Causation contested — Treisman (2007) finds negligible links; a "chicken-and-egg" problem. Preference heterogeneity reaches fullest meaning under democracy (Faguet 2014).
- **Economic growth:** mostly studied in reverse (decentralisation's impact on growth). Growth might fuel centralisation (welfare spending) or decentralisation (empowered local electorates); the **new economic geography** ("untraded interdependencies," Storper 1997) suggests it is the character of modern, knowledge-cluster development — not growth per se — that drives regional autonomy claims (Keating 2013).

These dynamic drivers yield **even less certainty** than static determinants.

## The political economy of decentralisation

Decentralisation policy emerges from intricate negotiation between central and local powers.

- **Riker (1964):** federalism's political arithmetic — when national leaders owe their position to regional "barons," power flows outward (decentralisation); when regional authorities serve at the centre's pleasure, autonomy withers (Rodríguez-Pose & Gill 2003).
- **Left–right divide:** the left, wedded to macroeconomic management and redistribution, leans centralist; the right associates decentralisation with privatisation. Reality is ironic — in Britain it was **Labour** that championed devolution while the Conservatives resisted.
- **Electoral cycles:** a new government built in the periphery can open a brief window for radical reform; opposition parties locked out of the centre seek regional/local influence (Keating 2013).
- **Other lenses:** the waning of national class-based politics; alignment of local business with regional politicians; the **Leviathan theory of government** (Brennan & Buchanan 1980) — tiers maximising their own power, not public welfare.

Political economy fills a void in fiscal federalism's framework but alone cannot fully explain decentralisation's recent rise.

## Historical factors and decentralisation

Singular historical moments shape governance. Germany illustrates history's heavy hand — world wars carved channels of decentralisation while the Nazi Party triggered a centralising spasm. War summons centralising forces (Beramendi 2012). Financial crises too: Rodríguez-Pose & Gill (2005) trace the 1970s economic storms to Thatcher's centralising Britain. Yet such moments often merely crystallise gradual processes. Panizza (1999) hypothesises that deviations from a fiscal-federalism "optimal" level of decentralisation represent history's interference — "historical errors" gradually corrected by democratic rational choice. These frameworks bridge econometric precision and historical narrative.

## Conclusion

As the twentieth century dawned, many saw centralisation as modernity's inevitable companion — **'Popitz's law'** (Popitz 1927, 1932), named for a Prussian finance minister, observed gradual centralisation in Switzerland, the US, Argentina, and Canada. But the later 20th and early 21st centuries reveal a decentralising tide and the emergence of **centrifugal states**.

The paper offers no new empirical insights but brings diverse indicators together. The accumulated research points to **constitutional federalism and country size** as decentralisation's most reliable predictors. Time's influence is more elusive; even economic growth's role faces theoretical and empirical challenges. The causal web links globalisation, growth, structural change, inequality, and democracy, whose temporal coincidence makes single-cause claims tempting but unreliable. Given data-coverage and reliability limits and the difficulty of reconciling general and specific causal systems, the drivers of decentralisation can at best **partially** explain it. Continued research is needed — intellectual humility required.
