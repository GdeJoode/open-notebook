## Preceding review fragment (top of page 3): Fritsch on Schumpeter

> This is the conclusion of an earlier review — *The Theory of Economic Development – An Inquiry into Profits, Capital, Credit, Interest, and the Business Cycle* (Schumpeter, 1934 ed.), reviewed by **Michael Fritsch** (Friedrich Schiller University Jena). DOI 10.1080/00343404.2017.1278975.

Schumpeter elaborated on the role of entrepreneurship for long-term economic development, stressing the challenge of innovation for established firms and industries, which he characterized as **"creative destruction"** (notably in *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy*, 1942). The "lost" seventh chapter of the 1911 first edition (later excluded, repr. Schumpeter 2002) delved into psychology and sociology to explore human motivations and the role of "milieu"/"culture", anticipating themes still relevant for explaining economic development.

The review credits Schumpeter with five contributions: (1) identifying innovation as a key source of growth; (2) highlighting entrepreneurial leadership; (3) insisting that explaining development requires a dynamic approach (grounding later **evolutionary theory**); (4) pioneering a socio-economic approach accounting for non-economic factors (motivation, personality, culture, history); and (5) laying foundations for systems approaches (innovation systems, systems of entrepreneurship). Noted weaknesses: a narrow definition of entrepreneurship (cf. Fritsch, 2013, on non-high-tech entries spurring growth) and neglect of the role of knowledge. Reviewer ORCiD: Michael Fritsch.

## Main review: Brakman & Garretsen on Kaldor's *Economics without equilibrium*

**Book reviewed:** *Economics without equilibrium*, Nicholas Kaldor, Sharpe, New York, 1985 (The Arthur M. Okun Memorial Lectures). 96 pp., ISBN 0906449871 (hbk).

### Introduction: Kaldor and the Okun Lectures

**Nicholas Kaldor (1908–86)** was one of the leading economists in Britain through much of the 20th century, especially after the Second World War, with a reputation extending well beyond the UK. The book collects three lectures given at **Yale University in 1983** in honour of **Arthur Okun**. The lectures provide a lucid summary of Kaldor's main contributions and, together with Kaldor (1986), can be seen as his last major academic statement. More than 30 years on, his predictions about where economic theory should go have to some extent been "picked up" by the profession. Were he alive, Kaldor would likely still be critical of mainstream economics' (mis)use of the **equilibrium** concept and other key ingredients of **neo-classical theory**.

Kaldor became Fellow at King's College, Cambridge (1949) and professor there (1966); his most influential work dates from his Cambridge years. He favoured the **inductive research method** — first establishing empirical regularities (his famous **"stylized facts"**) and only then building a theoretical model — in contrast to the **deductive approach** that still dominates economics. This places him in the **Marshallian tradition** (cf. Rodrik, 2015). His "stylized facts" are summaries of empirical regularities capturing fundamental truths, though potentially inaccurate in detail (Kaldor, 1957).

Writing on imperfect competition, business cycles, income distribution, growth theory, and capital measurement (the "capital controversy"), Kaldor is best known as a fierce critic of mainstream/neo-classical theory and as a founder of **post-Keynesian economics**, alongside his Cambridge colleague **Joan Robinson (1903–83)**. Post-Keynesian economics is heterogeneous, but its common theme is critique of neo-classical theory's emphasis on equilibrium. Kaldor considered the equilibrium concept fundamentally flawed — not abstraction itself, but **general equilibrium theory**, which he held starts with the wrong kind of abstraction.

The three lectures, each a chapter, are: (1) "Stylized Facts as a Basis for Theory Building"; (2) "Supply and Demand"; (3) "Interregional Trade and Cumulative Causation". The lecture format let Kaldor present his thoughts forcefully, without the constraints of journal refereeing.

### First lecture — critique of the equilibrium concept

Kaldor revisits his objections to the assumptions needed to produce equilibrium in a **(Walrasian) general equilibrium** setting. He stresses **price setting** (vs. price taking), the often-misleading role prices play in markets, expectations, and inventories. He is especially critical of the assumption that prices carry all relevant and correct information: prices can be subject to bubbles and speculation. General equilibrium theory assumes dealers (market-makers) and speculators "smooth out" (p. 17) bubbles, but Kaldor argues this is factually wrong — **markets usually do not clear**; transactions occur at different prices, times, and places, and the information carried by prices is inherently imperfect, making equilibrium "illusionary".

Knowledge differs among agents, magnifying the likelihood of non-clearing markets. Kaldor gives many examples of market failures. A telling one — also discussed by Okun in *Prices and Quantities* (1981) — is **Broadway theatre tickets**: even for a very popular show, theatres typically do not raise prices (an argument against profit maximization), and "fairness" enters the transaction (holding tickets for valued customers to avoid losing goodwill). His conclusion: such transactions cannot be reduced to "simple formulae" (p. 26) because "sellers are price-makers and quantity takers, and not, as Walrasian equilibrium theory supposes, price-takers and quantity makers" (p. 31).

The reviewers note mainstream theory has since absorbed much of this criticism — models of imperfect competition with price-setting firms, imperfect information, self-fulfilling expectations, and fairness/reputation are now common — yet at a fundamental level little has changed: these are still analyzed within equilibrium, clearing-market frameworks with marginal-cost pricing, which Kaldor firmly rejected.

### Second lecture — imperfect competition and heterogeneous firms

The aim is not definitive answers but to "point up the areas of our ignorance" (p. 32): the lack of analytical handling of the real-world presence of imperfect competition and **heterogeneous firms**. Kaldor (writing before firm microdata were widely available) speculates about differing profit shares across firms and the consequences for income distribution. Drawing on his work since the first **monopolistic competition revolution** of the 1930s, he reflects on the pricing behaviour of heterogeneous firms in imperfect markets, struggling especially with the fact that prices "maintain a fairly constant mark-up relationship to costs".

A **second monopolistic competition revolution** had just begun with the **Dixit–Stiglitz model (1977)**, which formally solved problems Kaldor was raising (constant mark-up, Marshall's representative firm, increasing returns, equilibrium). This culminated in the **Melitz (2003) model** introducing **firm heterogeneity** into the Dixit–Stiglitz framework, rendering invalid Kaldor's complaint (p. 46) that imperfect competition with a hierarchy of varying-cost firms had never been generally considered. Still, the reviewers note, these remain equilibrium models with profit-maximizing firms and informationally correct prices — so Kaldor would give only "lukewarm applause".

### Third lecture — interregional trade and cumulative causation

The most interesting lecture for *Regional Studies* readers, on **"Interregional Trade and Cumulative Causation"**. It opens with criticism of formal modelling prevailing over empirical regularities; the reviewers note the **Great Recession of 2008** re-energized this debate (Rodrik, 2015). Kaldor cites **Marshall** (pp. 58–59) and the view (also Krugman, 1998) that formal/mathematical models should be a means, never an end.

Kaldor revisits **increasing returns**, arguing (echoing **Myrdal**) that increasing returns drive the process of **cumulative causation** and can lead to **regional divergence** (Kaldor, 1970). Thirlwall (2013), reviewing Kaldor (1970), argues Kaldor's work predates the **New Economic Geography (NEG)** initiated by **Krugman (1991a, 1991b)**, who cites Kaldor as a predecessor. Combining interregional trade and increasing returns, Kaldor concludes (quoted, p. 74) that under increasing returns one industrial centre grows faster, accelerating its productivity growth and export surplus — a self-reinforcing "push". To readers familiar with NEG and agglomeration economics this is familiar territory.

For those approaching regional growth from a non-mainstream perspective (economic geography, regional science, development studies, post-Keynesian economics), Kaldorian growth models and increasing returns always remained relevant — e.g., uneven regional development across the UK (Rowthorn, 2010; Gardiner, Martin, Sunley, & Tyler, 2013). Kaldor's work was extended by Cambridge "Kaldorian" economists (Tony Thirlwall, Bob Rowthorn, John McCombie, Mark Roberts). NEG incorporated only the formalizable aspects of Kaldor's thinking (Martin, 1999); less formalizable aspects remain ignored. One example: **Verdoorn's law** (labour productivity increases associated with output growth via increasing returns) is essential to Kaldor's growth model but has not entered mainstream agglomeration/growth models (McCombie & Roberts, 2007).

The partial, belated acceptance of Kaldor's increasing-returns ideas is summarized by Krugman (1995, p. 1244), recalling that early-1980s increasing-returns trade theory looked like a "grab-bag" opposed to the unity of constant-returns, perfect-competition trade theory, but soon cohered into a "quite simple common framework" — a "grammar" extending beyond standard trade analysis. The reviewers' point: Kaldor would most likely **reject** this framework, given his critique of the equilibrium concept underlying it — a critique extending to related neo-classical building blocks like **aggregate production functions** (Cobb–Douglas, CES), and **marginal productivity theory**.

### Conclusion

Re-reading the lectures three decades on is rewarding, especially for those interested in regional growth and development. Some of Kaldor's criticisms and suggestions have been taken on board (progress), but his more fundamental critique of economic theorizing still stands, since the dominant approach remains **"economics with equilibrium"**.
