## Abstract

Nation-states across the world are increasingly relinquishing powers to subnational tiers of government. This paper examines the drivers of this "decentralisation revolution" and the forces propelling it. While conventional wisdom has long attributed the phenomenon to the triumvirate of **democracy, economic efficiency, and regional identity**, contemporary dynamics demand a more nuanced interpretation. Through the dual lenses of the **efficiency-based decentralisation theorem** and **political economy analysis**, the paper discusses how globalisation, technological upheaval, and innovations in governance have reshaped the relationship between political centres and peripheries within countries. The empirical evidence reveals decentralisation not as a linear progression but as an intricate interweaving of economic imperatives and political will — a complexity that defies simple causation, suggesting the dispersal of power responds to "an elaborate choreography of classical and modern forces."

## Introduction

Since the 1970s a strong centrifugal force within the nation-state — manifesting as decentralisation — has become a defining feature of contemporary governance, reshaping polities "from Britain to Brazil, Spain to China." While global forces pull countries together (e.g. the European Union), internal pressures push toward fragmentation. The central question: **why is decentralisation happening globally, and why now?**

Three "grand narratives" have traditionally explained transfers of authority from central to subnational tiers: the **democratic imperative**, the **pursuit of economic efficiency**, and the **persistence of regional identity** (citing Treisman 2007, Beramendi 2012, Faguet 2014; Rodríguez-Pose & Ezcurra 2010, Hooghe & Marks 2013; McGarry & O'Leary, Keating 2013). Yet appeals to democracy, welfare, or ethnic identity often serve as "convenient justifications for policies implemented for altogether different reasons." The supposedly immutable division between **federal and unitary states** has given way to a fluid continuum.

The author identifies two leading analytical frameworks: one grounded in **welfare economics** (optimal service delivery, economic scale, territorial expanse), and one rooted in **political economy** (decentralisation as the product of competing interests and actors' ambitions). Determinants are classed as **static** (national size, population distribution, ethnic composition, constitutional traditions — explaining why federal India differs from centralised France) and **dynamic** (security threats, regional disparities, economic integration, the rise of the knowledge economy).

## The Political Economy Framework

The dominant theory has been the **"decentralisation theorem"** (Oates 1972, 2005), distilling the **"efficiency approach"** of Samuelson (1954, 1955), Tiebout (1956), Musgrave (1959), and Arrow (1969). It matches governmental competencies to their optimal territorial scale, weighing economies of scale (favouring centralisation, e.g. national defence) against the benefits of tailoring services to local preferences (e.g. waste collection, employment training). Under specific conditions, varied local provision is **Pareto superior** to uniform central delivery (Oates 2005). Reframed via voter/investor preferences (Rodden & Wibbels 2010) and policymakers' pursuit of welfare (Weingast 2014), it becomes explanatory as well as normative.

**Critiques:** The theorem falters on political reality. Rodden (2006) dismisses the idea that decentralisation flows naturally from welfare economics. Garman et al. (2001) stress the negotiation among politicians, bureaucrats, and interest groups. Treisman (2007): "even if fiscal decentralisation is more efficient in ethnically divided societies, that does not imply that ethnically divided societies would tend to be more fiscally decentralised." Even Oates (2005) called for a **"second-generation" theory of fiscal federalism**.

Two observations: (1) decentralisation's various forms spring from different sources (e.g. the flowering of nation-states after the Berlin Wall — Alesina & Spolaore 2003); (2) centralisation and decentralisation **coexist as "two faces of the same political coin"** — Blankart (2000) notes decentralisation often emerges as a reaction to over-centralisation, and the "decentralisation pendulum" swings constantly.

## Static Determinants of Decentralisation

Static factors are like "photographs" (vs. dynamic "films"): wealth, territorial expanse, population, ethnic composition.

- **Constitutions / federalism:** Change at a glacial pace (Escobar-Lemmon 2003). Federal constitutions generally herald higher decentralisation, but the federal/unitary binary "dissolves into a continuum" (Keating 2013); Germany can be more centralised than France in some areas, and France has quietly decentralised (Jeffery 2015). Treisman (2007) suggests the correlation may better be understood via economic development.
- **Colonial heritage:** British colonial administration planted seeds of local autonomy (Hadiz 2010); many Spanish-Latin American domains remain centralised, with federal constitutions in Argentina, Mexico, Venezuela sometimes "mere facades" (Falleti 2010).
- **Preference heterogeneity:** Fiscal federalism predicts decentralisation flourishes where regional voter preferences vary markedly (Hooghe et al. 2010).
- **Size & population density:** Larger nations incline toward decentralisation (Panizza 1999; Arzaghi & Henderson 2005; Treisman 2007); distance is a barrier to unified governance (Lessmann & Markwardt 2010). Sparse populations may demand decentralisation; concentrated urban populations achieve economies of scale.
- **Ethnic diversity:** Relationship is ambiguous. Some states use decentralisation as a "pressure valve" for ethnic tensions (Brancati 2009); yet Treisman (2007) finds ethnic fractionalisation sometimes correlates with **centralisation**; Arzaghi & Henderson (2005) suggest diversity may lead governments to restrict decentralisation to limit separatism.
- **Wealth & inequality:** Regional income disparities can favour decentralisation (Lessmann 2012) but also strengthen the centre's redistributive role (Kyriacou & Roca-Sagalés 2011). National wealth generally correlates positively with decentralisation (Panizza 1999; Letelier-Saavedra & Sáez-Lozano 2015).

**Summary:** Larger, more populous, wealthier nations and those with federal constitutions tend toward decentralisation (Rodríguez-Pose & Vidal-Bover 2024); ethnic diversity, inequality, and wealth tell more complex, context-mediated tales.

## Dynamic Drivers of Decentralisation

- **Globalisation** (Ezcurra & Rodríguez-Pose 2013) and **democratisation** (Pius Kulipossa 2004; Enikolopov & Zhuravskaya 2007) are "twin forces."
- **Economic integration:** International market integration makes "smallness in government less costly" (Rodrik 2011/2012). The **EU** is the foremost test case — multilevel governance, regions with Brussels outposts under "Europe of the Regions" (Hooghe & Marks 2016; Keating 2013). Yet evidence is ambiguous: some find positive correlations (Stegarescu 2009; Kyriacou & Roca-Sagalés 2011), while Diaz-Cayeros (2006) found trade openness walking with **centralisation**, and Rodrik argues globalisation's risks demand stronger central coordination.
- **Democratisation:** Democracy expanded dramatically 1974–2012 (World Bank 2012), coinciding with decentralisation in Spain (Colino 2013), Eastern Europe (Fritz et al. 2009), and Latin America (Falleti 2010). Exceptions: Estonia's post-democratic centralisation, Zimbabwe, Belarus. Preference heterogeneity reaches its "fullest meaning under democracy." Faguet (2014) sees decentralisation as both democracy's "handmaiden and redemption song." Regional resistance to authoritarian regimes can feed decentralisation (Brancati 2009; Brazil — Montero 2007). Causation remains contested; Treisman (2007) finds negligible links — a "chicken-and-egg problem."
- **Economic growth:** Mostly studied as a consequence of decentralisation. Growth may fuel centralisation (welfare spending) or decentralisation (empowered local electorates); empirical studies generally favour the latter (Rodríguez-Pose & Ezcurra 2010; Lessmann & Markwardt 2010). The **new economic geography** ("untraded interdependencies," Storper 1997) suggests it is the character of modern, knowledge-clustered development — not growth per se — that drives regional autonomy demands (Keating 2013).

Dynamic drivers yield **even less certainty** than static determinants.

## The Political Economy of Decentralisation

Oates (2005) acknowledged fiscal federalism's blind spot: silence on political machinations. Politicians' ambitions shape devolved power.

- **Riker (1964) theory of federalism:** Where party members/voters elect regional leaders who then choose the national leader, power trickles outward (decentralisation); where they elect a national "sovereign" who dispenses regional authority, the system tends toward centralisation (Rodríguez-Pose & Gill 2003).
- **Left–right divide:** Left parties (macroeconomic management, redistribution) gravitate toward centralisation; the right toward decentralisation (privatisation, less state oversight). Counter-narrative: in **Britain, Labour championed devolution** while Conservatives resisted.
- **Electoral cycles:** A new government built in the periphery before "conquering the centre" creates a brief reform window (Keating 2013), but central power dulls the appetite for decentralisation; opposition parties seek regional/local influence.
- **Other lenses:** Local-suited challenges (environment, social services — John 2014); waning class-based politics; alignment of local business and regional politicians; the **Leviathan theory of government** (Brennan & Buchanan 1980), where each tier maximises its own power and resources.

Political economy fills a void in fiscal federalism but alone cannot fully explain decentralisation's recent prominence.

## Historical Factors and Decentralisation

History's "singular moments" matter. The **German narrative**: the two world wars carved channels of decentralisation, while the Nazi Party triggered a "violent centralising spasm." War invariably summons centralising forces (Beramendi 2012). Financial crises too: Rodríguez-Pose & Gill (2005) trace how the 1970s economic storms birthed the centralising impulses of **Thatcher's Britain**. Panizza (1999) hypothesises that deviations from fiscal federalism's "optimal" decentralisation represent **history's interference** — "historical errors" that gradually yield to democratic rational choice.

## Conclusion

As the 20th century dawned, scholars saw centralisation as modernity's inevitable companion — **"Popitz's law"** (Popitz 1927, 1932), named for a Prussian finance minister, observed gradual centralisation in Switzerland, the US, Argentina, and Canada. Yet the late 20th and early 21st centuries have witnessed a decentralising tide: the emergence of **"centrifugal states."**

The accumulated research points to **constitutional federalism and country size** as decentralisation's most reliable predictors. Time's influence is more elusive; even economic growth's role faces theoretical and empirical challenges. The causal web of globalisation, growth, structural change, inequality, and democracy, and the persistence of contradictory cases, demand "intellectual humility." The author offers **no new empirical insights** but brings together diverse indicators rarely considered in concert. Researchers must keep filling blank spaces, knowing decentralisation can be only partially explained — "each study will add another important piece of this intricate puzzle of governance and power distribution."
